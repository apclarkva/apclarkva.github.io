Vision

From gene-by-gene to network-level

Arrhythmias come from the interaction between tissue structure and the electrical identity of individual cardiomyocytes. Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) build that identity by setting the expression of ion channels, calcium-handling proteins, and connexins.

Cardiomyocyte transcriptional regulators can remodel whole modules of the electrical program, and the effect of any perturbation depends on developmental stage and cell state. Even so, these links have mostly been worked out one gene at a time, without asking where each gene sits in the larger network.

My lab will work at the network level. The long-term objective is to understand how gene regulatory networks establish and maintain cardiomyocyte electrophysiology, and how changes to these networks cause pathogenic remodeling.

One loop for both areas

Computational and experimental models are matched. Model perturbations (DynaGRIN), single-cell phenotyping (CARBONITE), and genetic or drug perturbations feed each other.

Foundation

Eight years of building the tools to ask this question

Ph.D. · Cornell · David Christini

Single-cell measurement recovers what population averages hide

I combined mathematical modeling with patch-clamp experiments in human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) to work out how ionic currents shape electrophysiology. I built an in silico–in vitro pipeline that uses genetic algorithms to design voltage-clamp protocols that show which ion channels a drug blocks. With it, I found that quinine blocks the funny current, which had not been recognized before.

I also showed that these protocols explain the action potential heterogeneity that distorts recordings in the literature (Europace 2023, Adv Sci 2025) and limits iPSC-CMs as a model system more generally (AJP-Heart 2024, J Physiol 2024).

Six first-author papers · NIH F31 predoctoral fellowship

Postdoc · UVA · Jeffrey Saucerman

The networks that build cardiomyocyte identity

I developed complementary experimental (CARBONITE) and computational (DynaGRIN) approaches to perturb these networks and measure the consequences. Using DynaGRIN to nominate regulators and CARBONITE to test them, I found that MEF2C, PURA, and RREB1 are required during the short developmental window when early cardiomyocytes take on chamber-specific identity.

With human geneticists at UVA (Clint Miller, Saurabh Kulkarni), I found that all three genes are loss-of-function intolerant, conserved across species, and required for heart looping in Xenopus.

With Benoit Bruneau and Irfan Kathiriya (Gladstone/UCSF), we showed that MEF2C controls segment-specific GRNs in heart-tube morphogenesis (Genes Dev 2025) and mapped the GRNs destabilized by reduced TBX5 dosage in human atrial iPSC-CMs (Development 2026). I also built dynamic Netflux models of how perturbations spread through cardiomyocyte signaling networks.

Platforms

Two platforms I built

DynaGRIN

A general method that systematically perturbs scRNA-seq/scATAC-seq-inferred networks in silico to identify the transcription factors and regulons that control cell identity. Applied to heart-field GRNs, simulated knockdowns predicted 32 TFs that drive atrial vs. ventricular identity: 20 atrial (e.g., Nr2f2, Tbx5) and 12 ventricular (e.g., Hey2, Mef2c).

CARBONITE

CARBONITE pairs live calcium imaging with post hoc immunostaining of the same cells, linking function and molecular identity at single-cell resolution. In iPSC-CMs it revealed spatially organized functional states.

The method is now used in collaborations across BME and Cell Biology at UVA: in cardiomyocytes (Jeffrey Saucerman), neurons (Sameer Bajikar), endothelial cells (Karen Hirschi), and iPSCs (Eli Zunder).

Area 1 How the program is built

Gene regulatory network control of cardiomyocyte electrical identity

model → predict → validate

My postdoc work suggests that cardiomyocyte electrical identity is set through discrete transitions in the earliest stages of heart formation. I identified TFs and regulatory networks that control two of them: the emergence of a cardiomyocyte from a cardiac progenitor, and the conversion of atrial and ventricular molecular identity into distinct electrophysiology.

Many of these TFs are linked to morphogenetic defects in early heart development, but their roles in setting heart rhythm are unknown. A recurring feature is dosage sensitivity: humans do not tolerate loss-of-function mutations in even one copy of the gene.

Central hypothesis Mutations in early, dosage-sensitive heart-forming TFs create cell-autonomous rhythm disturbances, independent of structural malformation.
1a  progenitor → beating cardiomyocyte
1b  chamber identity → chamber function

1a How do gene regulatory networks build an electrically active cardiomyocyte?

  • MEF2C
  • PURA
  • RREB1

As the heart starts to take shape during the cardiac crescent-to-heart-tube transition, the first cardiomyocytes appear and begin to beat. My preliminary work suggests that newly active cardiomyocytes pass through a plastic, chamber-agnostic electrical state before atrial and ventricular electrophysiology diverge. Perturbing specific TFs (MEF2C, PURA, RREB1) during this transition strongly alters calcium handling.

My lab will look earlier. My GRNs and public snRNA-seq data suggest these genes are already expressed at the progenitor stage. We will use DynaGRIN to perturb these regulators in cardiac progenitor GRNs and predict changes to calcium and ion-channel genes, then test those perturbations in action potential models. Longitudinal calcium and voltage imaging of differentiating iPSC-CMs will catch the first spontaneous beat of a cardiomyocyte, and CARBONITE will link each newly active cell to its molecular identity.

Follow-on studies will look for dosage-sensitive regulators through which congenital heart disease mutations could create an arrhythmic substrate before, or independent of, structural malformation.

1b How do gene regulatory networks turn chamber identity into chamber function?

  • NR2F2
  • HEY2

Atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes establish distinct molecular identities before they acquire distinct electrophysiology. Human iPSC-CMs show atrial- and ventricular-specific molecular signatures by about day 20, but their calcium transients are still similar. Over the next seven days, their function splits into distinct atrial and ventricular calcium phenotypes.

I hypothesize that chamber-specifying TFs (e.g., NR2F2, HEY2) start a regulatory program that drives distinct ion-channel and calcium-handling machinery in each chamber. My lab will apply DynaGRIN to human fetal, human iPSC-CM, and mouse fetal GRNs to find regulators predicted to drive this divergence. We will build those predictions into mechanistic atrial and ventricular iPSC-CM action potential models, then perturb high-priority regulators as chamber-specific function emerges. Longitudinal imaging and endpoint CARBONITE will match each cell's emerging function with its molecular identity.

Area 2 Why individual cells fail

Single-cardiomyocyte determinants of electrical vulnerability

measure → encode → constrain

Protein expression and electrophysiological function vary from cell to cell in every biological system, even within one cell type. That variability is often useful, but in the heart it can go wrong and cause lethal arrhythmias.

With only calcium dynamics and a few protein markers, CARBONITE found relationships among molecular identity, cellular neighborhood, and electrical function that could not be seen when each modality was measured separately. Later perturbation studies linked variation in regulatory state, such as TF abundance and protein interactions, to variation in functional response.

Central hypothesis The covariance among transcriptional regulators and electrophysiological effectors sets a cardiomyocyte's electrical phenotype more than any single abundance does, and predicts which cells diverge under perturbation.

2a What features of a cell predict its function?

My lab will extend CARBONITE to measure voltage and calcium, followed by multiplexed molecular phenotyping of the same cells with iterative immunostaining. We will quantify TFs, ion channels, calcium-handling proteins, and structural features to build a joint molecular and electrophysiological profile for thousands of cardiomyocytes per experiment. We will test not only which proteins are present but how their abundance and subcellular organization predict function.

A new way to constrain models. Current population-of-models approaches vary ionic conductances across assumed distributions and treat parameters as largely independent. Instead, I will use measured covariance between regulators, effectors, and function to build molecularly constrained populations of models. Then I will test whether these better predict cell-to-cell variability in repolarization and calcium handling, and identify which molecular features make individual cells vulnerable.

2b Can we predict which cells are vulnerable to delayed proarrhythmic remodeling?

Preclinical proarrhythmia assessment is good at catching drugs that directly perturb cardiac ion channels. It falls short when drugs indirectly remodel cardiomyocyte state, as cancer therapies that target transcriptional and signaling machinery do. HDAC and CDK4/6 inhibitors, for example, may change ion-channel expression and alter repolarization beyond acute channel block. These effects appear over hours to days, so conventional acute assays capture them poorly.

I hypothesize that cancer therapeutics create arrhythmic vulnerability by remodeling regulatory state, and that the effect depends on each cell's molecular state before and during treatment. We will expose human iPSC-CMs to transcription-targeting cancer drugs for days to weeks, follow each cell's electrical trajectory with longitudinal imaging, and use CARBONITE to find the TF and ion-channel changes that explain it. Candidate mechanisms will be tested causally by genetic perturbation.

Outcome: mechanisms of remodeling-driven proarrhythmia, the cell states most vulnerable to it, and a new link to cardio-oncology.

Significance & outlook

A collaborative, integrative lab

My lab will train undergraduates, graduate students, postdocs, and scientists from different disciplines in an environment built around collaborative, integrative work on hard biological problems.

By combining New Approach Methods (human-based and computational models) with systems bioengineering to study both development and acquired disease, the program can compete across many funding mechanisms. The cancer-therapy remodeling studies also connect to NCI and NHLBI priorities in cardio-oncology and treatment-related cardiotoxicity.
